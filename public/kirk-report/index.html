Part two

What the machines see, and why it matters

You live in the middle of the AI boom. Half your corporate clients are building it. Here is the part that matters for a charter business: the way people find businesses like yours is changing, and it is changing fast.

For twenty years, the game was ranking on Google. A customer typed "sailing san francisco," got ten blue links, and clicked one. That era is ending. More and more, there is no list at all. People ask ChatGPT or Claude a question in plain English, or Google itself answers with AI at the top of the page, and the customer gets one or two recommendations instead of ten options. If the AI knows your business, you get the call. If it can't read your website, you simply don't come up.

That's the whole story of this section. First, where things stand today. Then the numbers on how fast this shift is happening. Then exactly what I changed so the next recommendation is you.

I ran the test myself

I asked Claude, on a normal phone, where to go sailing in San Francisco. It recommended two of your competitors by name: Adventure Cat and SF Bay Adventures, "out of Sausalito for smaller-group sails."

The AI recommended someone else for small-group sails out of Sausalito. That is exactly what you are, and your reviews are better than theirs (5.0 across three platforms, #1 of 42 things to do in Sausalito).

The AI isn't playing favorites. It can only work with what it can read, and the old site gives it almost nothing to read. Every change in this section exists to fix precisely this: the structured business data, the plain-text prices, the machine-readable FAQ. The goal is simple: the next time someone asks that question, the answer includes Captain Kirk's.

What I actually did about it

  • A machine-readable business card on every page. Hidden in the code (the industry term is "structured data" or "schema"), every page now carries a precise, standardized description of your business: name, phone, address, hours, prices, boats, the 2002 founding date, your Cemetery & Funeral Bureau license number. Humans never see it. Every AI and search engine does.
  • Your FAQ is now machine-answerable. All 18 questions and answers are marked up in the format Google and AI assistants ingest directly, so "can I bring my dog on a sailing charter in SF?" has one obvious source: you. (Answer aboard: yes, and you carry dog life jackets.)
  • Prices, durations, and capacities live in plain text. Sounds obvious, but two of your four main competitors publish no consumer prices at all, and a third contradicts itself between pages. An AI asked "how much does a private sail cost in San Francisco" can quote your site cleanly. Theirs, it can't.
  • Google's own case studies show pages with structured data earning 25 to 82% more clicks from the same search position.6

Housekeeping that protects your Google ranking

  • Your address appears three different ways across the internet (150, 180, and 310 Harbor Dr). Google reads that inconsistency as unreliability. The new site states one canonical address per boat, and I'll align your Google Business Profile at handoff.
  • Your current site has a broken secure-connection setup: typing "sfbaysail.com" routes visitors through an insecure hop before landing. Chrome has flagged such connections as "Not secure" since 2018.7 Nearly half of users say they won't enter personal details on a site flagged that way.8 The new site is fully secure from the first request, and I'll fix the domain routing at handoff.
What this earns you: when the fastest-growing discovery channel in travel recommends "a private sailing charter in San Francisco," yours is the business it can actually read, verify, and quote, while every competitor's site gives it nothing to work with.

Part five

From 69 pages to 12

Before the page-by-page tour, the biggest structural change deserves a proper explanation, because it's easy to miss: the old site had grown to 69 separate pages. About two dozen core pages accumulated over the years (four different boat pages, two separate trip lists, two testimonial pages), plus roughly 45 short blog posts, the newest from June 2024. None of it was wrong. It's just how a website grows when a busy owner adds one page at a time for twenty years, and the result was that a first-time visitor had no clear path, and your reputation was spread thin across dozens of small, overlapping pages.
