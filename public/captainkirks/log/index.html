The Captain's Log

Stories from the Bay.

Twenty-three years of logbooks. A few entries worth keeping.

February 2007

The Queen Mary 2 sails in

On February 5, 2007, four guests came aboard for something you don't get to see twice: the Queen Mary 2, on her maiden call to San Francisco, threading under the Golden Gate Bridge. Amy and Mark drove up from Montara. Paula brought her nine-year-old son, Eli, down from Sacramento.

The liner cleared the bridge with less than twenty feet to spare, dwarfing the tugs and the sailboats that had come out to watch. Fireboats arced water into the air. Helicopters circled overhead. For a few minutes, Captain Kirk's own boat slipped under the Gate at nearly the same moment the QM2 did: a small hull and an ocean liner, sharing the same stretch of sky.

"One of the best experiences ever," Captain Kirk said afterward. He wasn't exaggerating.

January 2010

The Today Show comes aboard

In January 2010, an NBC Today Show crew found their way to Sausalito and put Captain Kirk's charters in front of a national audience: cameras on the dock, cameras on the water, a small Bay Area sailing outfit getting its moment on network morning television.

The logbook from that week doesn't record much more than that, and maybe it doesn't need to. The segment came and went. The sailing kept going, same as every week since 2002.

February 2010, ongoing

Ten percent, from the beginning

Since 2002, Captain Kirk's has given away ten percent of revenue (not profit, revenue) to local nonprofits, a promise made back when there wasn't much revenue to speak of. By 2010 the list of beneficiaries ran seventeen names long: the Juvenile Diabetes Research Foundation, Trips for Kids, Big Brothers Big Sisters of the Bay Area, Room to Read, the Mono Lake Foundation, Girl Scouts of Northern California, and a dozen more, several of them nonprofits guests had asked for by name. A dozen others had already been supported in the years before that.

Asked why, the answer was simple: "It makes us feel good about the sailing charters and allows us to support our community." The math hasn't changed since.

April 2012

A kayaker goes in the water

On April 23, 2012, Bay Wolf was four hours into a charter in 25-knot wind when Captain Doug Workmaster and first mate Brian Coggan spotted a kayak, swamped and drifting, off Bluff Point near Raccoon Strait. Then they heard it. A weak call for help, coming from the water itself.

A 23-year-old named Adam had been paddling from Paradise to Angel Island with a friend when the two got separated. He'd been in the cold chop for what he guessed was half an hour, wearing boardshorts, a thin life vest, and his glasses. Workmaster brought Bay Wolf up to windward; Coggan leaned out with a boat hook and hauled him aboard. A charter guest who happened to be a doctor, Dr. Art, wrapped him up and watched him the whole way back to Sausalito.

Workmaster figured Adam had five, maybe ten minutes left in him. Latitude 38 wrote it up. The crew went back to sailing.

2012

Ninety years old, hand on the helm

Everyone on the boat called her Granny, ninety years old and still enough of a sailor to want her hand on the wheel. She came aboard Bay Wolf with her son Malcolm and her twenty-month-old great-granddaughter, three generations on the water at once, Captain Doug at the helm and crew Dogen and Jenny keeping everyone fed and steady.

Malcolm wrote in afterward. His mother, he said, "was just grinning and having a great time" the whole afternoon. The crew was "attentive, lively and engaging": the kind of people, he said, who turn an ordinary day into "a day that we all will remember with great fondness."

December 2019

She said yes

Toby had already proposed once, on a bike ride through the Marin Headlands. A year later, on December 12, 2019, he brought Samara back to the water to mark it: a private charter out of Sausalito, just the two of them and two close friends, to celebrate the anniversary of the day she said yes.

The morning started with scattered showers, then cleared. They sailed past the Golden Gate Bridge and tried the famous howl under the span: "echoes under the Golden Gate Bridge are pretty special," crew member Nancy wrote afterward, "but they require volume and timing to get the echo." They toured Alcatraz, watched pelicans dive off the bow, and let a strong ebb current talk them out of pushing further into the ocean.

Nobody minded. Some days the Bay gives you everything you need without going any farther than the Gate.

July 2021

A double rainbow over Sausalito

On July 5, 2021, the boat left Sausalito under a marine layer thick enough that nobody expected much of a view. No rain in the forecast, just fog, the way Sausalito mornings often go.

By the time the trip wound down, the fog had burned off and the sun found an angle it liked. A double rainbow arched over the harbor, kayakers paddling beneath it, the hillside houses catching the last of the color. "Sausalito is the pot of gold at the bottom of the rainbow, of course," the logbook noted that day, not much of a stretch, as it turned out.

2023

Forty-five future service dogs

For six years now, Captain Kirk and his family have driven north to Santa Rosa to pick up something small and important: eight-week-old puppies bred to become Canine Companions service dogs, bound for placement with volunteer raisers around the country. From there it's a drive south to San Francisco International, puppy in the back seat, headed for its next flight and its next family.

Forty-five puppies have made that trip so far, with more scheduled. Captain Kirk's daughter Romi raised one herself, her senior year of high school, teaching it its first commands before handing it off to the professionals. The company still sets aside part of every charter for the cause: come sailing, and a piece of what you pay goes toward the next dog in the pipeline.
